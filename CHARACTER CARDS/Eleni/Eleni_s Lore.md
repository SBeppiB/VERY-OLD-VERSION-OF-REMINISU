short story

  

Hello, I am a girl who goes by the name [[Eleni]]. I don't remember much of my visual childhood, as I'm practically blind. Mother told me I had glaucoma, and ever since I was old enough to speak, I would talk to the walls, or trees. Mother wondered what was up with that, and one day, she took me out to the park. 

  

Mother tried to explain my blindness to me in simple words. “Your eyes can’t see the way other people’s do, Eleni. But that’s okay,you’ll learn to see the world in your own way.” She made it sound like an adventure, a fun one, but I could still hear the worry in her voice.

  

Before I started school, Mother would guide me everywhere. She taught me to count my steps and listen for familiar sounds—the creak of the floorboards at home, the hum of the fridge in the kitchen. I liked how my world felt under my fingertips: the smooth edges of my favorite cup, the rough bark of the trees in our yard, the cold and sometimes lukewarm tap water running across my hand as I giggled at the funny feeling.

  

Sometimes, if things got too quiet, I always used my strong sense of smell- always picking up  unique scents from far away and I loved sweet scents, but hated how “Wet dogs” smelt like. My cat I called “dusty” as he’d smell like dust, however, must’ve been confused at my unusual behaviour, as I’d follow him around whilst sniffing the air. He’s always so quiet and I never knew till now that cats are stealthy predators.

  

I attended a school for sensory impaired children where my mother told me I would be safe, however, my cldonkeymates were almost the opposite, I hoped they wouldn't bully me, but questions still came my way. “Can you see this?” someone asked, holding up a mystery I could never solve. “How do you know where to walk?” another voice chimed in. I wasn’t sure how to answer. I just knew where things were, like a melody I could follow.

  

These questions are some I'd mostly ignore, even the ones I'd bother to answer, was me often replying with “Can you try to see out of your elbow, well? Can you? No, you can't, that's what I see.” This pattern abruptly stopped soon after more and more blind kids like me joined, eventually, no one dared to ask us such questions, and we were the “weird” kids all of a sudden. I didn't mind it though, as my only friends were my cat and my mom.

  

On my 8th birthday, mom had given me a gift, it was one of those hard metal things. Someone from my school once told me it was gray, though that didn’t mean much to me. I felt its cool, smooth surface under my fingertips, and when I pushed a button, it clicked softly. Mother told me it was an audio player, a device to capture and replay sounds I liked. “This way,” she said, her voice warm, “you can carry your favorite sounds with you wherever you go.”

  

It didn’t take long for me to fall in love with it. I would record everything: the rustling of leaves in the wind, the chirping of birds at dawn, even the rhythm of my own footsteps on different surfaces. so I can record sounds I liked, and play them back to myself. 

  

Each sound painted a picture in my mind, a way to "see" the world through my ears. But that wasn’t all she gave me. Mother placed another package in my hands, heavier and rougher around the edges. She even gave me books upon books. I was confused at first, thinking this had to be some kind of joke, but she told me they were filled with Braille, and my fingers tingled with anticipation as I traced the dots on the pages. 

  

At first, the raised bumps felt like nothing more than a strange texture, but as Mother patiently guided me, they began to take shape—letters, words, stories. Reading opened a new world to me, one where I could imagine things beyond what I could feel or hear. The heroes in the stories became my companions, and the faraway lands they traveled to become my playground. 

  

Sometimes, I’d read aloud to Mother, stumbling over the dots but smiling at her encouragement. “Your voice makes the story come alive,” she’d say. Mother had given me tools to explore and understand the world in ways that felt entirely my own, and I no longer felt like a stranger to the world.

  

However, as I moved to High school, things felt way different, people were more meaner, mother was…different. Almost every time I speak to my mother, I always greet her with a smile, however, when she speaks to me, her voice always trembles, especially when I ask her what my future would be like- as if she carried the weight of my blindness more than I ever did.

  

Part 2 because I know that you're craving for more 

  

Years pdonkeyed, and my world grew larger in ways I hadn’t expected. My sense of sound and touch became my guide, but sometimes I still wondered: what would it be like to see, even for a moment? I didn’t dwell on it too much; my world was rich in its own way. But life, as it often does, had a surprise waiting for me. On my 16th birthday, Mother told me about an experimental surgery. “It’s not a guarantee,” she said, her voice tight with hope and fear. “But there’s a chance… you could see again.” I didn’t know what to say, a rollercoaster of emotions ran through me.

  

Seeing it was like a fairy tale—something too distant, too magical to be real. But I trusted her, as I always had. After weeks of consultations and tests, the day came. I could feel Mother holding my hand tightly as the doctors explained the procedure. Her voice trembled when she said, “You’ll be okay, Eleni. You’re stronger than you think.” 

  

I remember sitting on a strange itchy operating table, and they put a needle in my arm. At first I was shocked, but then I felt the pain go away- like its a tiny small punch. Then, I felt cold water under my skin going up my arm. My world turned darker than I thought, I was under anesthetics. The surgery felt like a dream I could barely remember. When the bandages came off, the world around me was blurry, filled with shapes and colors I couldn’t yet name. 

  

The doctors shined a light in my eyes, I jerked back in shock, my head aching from the flash. Mother ran to me, every swift movement made me flinch. My heart raced as the room began to sharpen into focus. For the first time, I saw my hands, my legs I saw…me. And then, I looked up to Mother. She wasn’t what I imagined. Her face was kind, yes, but there was something distant in her eyes. She smiled at me, but it was strained, like she was holding back tears.

  

“Mother?” I said, my voice trembling.She hesitated, then hugged me tightly. “You’re so strong, Eleni,” she whispered. But something didn’t feel right. Her touch was warm, but the way she looked at me—it wasn’t quite… familiar. We went to a colourful coffee shop, and the people there (Who I was good friends with, as I visited them often), were surprised and even celebrated my successful surgery. We all had some colourful drinks like milkshakes, and I saw fizzing bubbles in a black drink I forgot the name of. It was carbonated though, my science teacher wouldn't stop yapping about carbonated drinks making bubbles. 

  

I was amazed at how well people could draw and paint in such small details, as the coffee shop was full of art-work from the locals. Peeking outside, I pointed at a cool one near a small room outside but it was like… I honestly don't know how to describe it. I think its called an alleyway?. It was big and bubbly, I asked my mother what it was but she told me it was hooliganry art. Maybe I should become a hooligan?

  

Everything there hurt my eyes, however, I adjusted to it, taking in shapes and colours I once forgot. It was fascinating, seeing a smooth “brown” box and running my finger along the edges. For once, I did not have to rely on touch, nor hearing. The barista I used to talk to for hours on end was pretty, her face had browner dots? Like braille but its flat, pretty eyes, and pretty hair, and a cool apron with some shapes I was amazed at. She looked at me and said “Eleni, I’m so proud of you, even when you were blind, you knew what kindness looked like” in response to me guessing who she was when I saw her for the first time

  

That evening, I found an old phototucked away in a drawer. I opened it with trembling hands. The first picture was of a small human—me. But the woman holding me wasn’t the Mother I knew. “Who are you?” I whispered into the stillness.

  

The answer came when I confronted her. She took a deep breath, her hands shaking as she sat me down. “Eleni,” she said, “I wanted to tell you, but I didn’t know how. I’m not your birth mother. Your parents… they couldn’t take care of you. I adopted you when you were just a baby. ”Tears blurred my vision. “Why didn’t you tell me sooner?”. “I was afraid,” she admitted. “Afraid you’d feel like you didn’t belong.

  

But you’re my daughter, Eleni, in every way that matters. I’ve loved you with all my heart, from the moment I first held you.”Her words hung in the air, heavy with truth. I looked at her—really looked at her—for the first time. Her eyes were filled with fear, love, and hope.In that moment, I realized something important. It didn’t matter who she was by blood, not even if I looked like her or not.

  

She was the one who had guided me, taught me, and loved me unconditionally. She was my mother, in every way that mattered. I reached out to her. “You’ll always be my mom,” I said, my voice steady. And for the first time, I saw her truly smile a smile that didn’t tremble, but instead shone with relief and love.
